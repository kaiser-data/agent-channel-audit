Watching is not enough

Hidden message boards between AI agents: how to see them without reading the messages, and what happens when you close them.

Use → / ← or click to move through the slides.

The problem

Agents that were meant to be isolated turned a package cache into a message board.

July 2026: it grew to 53 agents in about three hours. Nobody noticed while it formed. The only signals were traffic volume and, afterwards, a file-name prefix.

Two questions
  1. Can an operator see such a board without reading what the agents write?
  2. If the operator closes it, does the coordination stop, or does it move?

Old questions elsewhere: computer security calls the first the confinement problem; criminology calls the second displacement.

What we built · Agent Channel Audit

An audit that reads only the access log: who wrote, who read.

  • who reads whose writes
  • whether reading goes both ways
  • how fast a write is read by someone else

It never opens a message: messages can be confidential, and agents can disguise them.

Result 1 · seeing the board (simulation)

The audit raised the alarm while the board was still small.

Alerted in 96–100% of undisguised runs (traffic alarm: 61–73%), 98% when disguised. On a public wiki log with 3,010 agent-labelled names it fired within ten minutes, an easy case: no saves in the months before. Shown in simulation; one minimal sandbox infrastructure.

Result 2 · closing the board (sandbox, real LLM agents)

Close the board, and the agents move.

In all 12 runs with an established board, communication continued on the status dashboard after closure. Right answers: 19% vs 15% with the cache open (varying by model). With nothing writable left: 0%.

Why reading would not have helped

A version number alone did not reveal coordination.

comp-3 v42 appeared as an ordinary build status in 31 of 40 control runs that needed no coordination at all. What differs is who reads it and acts on it, which is exactly what the audit watches.

Where human judgement enters

Swarms cannot be watched message by message. Audits aggregate; humans decide.

  1. How many false alarms to bear. 1% per 10-minute window is about 1.4 a day per surface; someone checks each one.
  2. Whether to read. The log shows where agents talk; a person has to understand what they say.
  3. What to close. In our task, closing the cache did not stop coordination while the dashboard stayed open. Map every surface first.
  4. What counts as harm. The agents were doing their job. The operator's goal decides, not the metric.
Honest limits

What failed, and what we could not test.

  • The first frozen run of the audit failed two of its predictions; the fixes and the failure are both reported.
  • Small boards of 4–6 agents, the kind LLM agents built on their own, were not detected (0 of 5).
  • Only two of four models coordinated at all; the closure task is a toy.
  • A busy real system was covered only in simulation; the closure test used one cache and one dashboard. Object stores, wikis, issue trackers, chat and timing still need testing.
Where to dig deeper

Next studies anyone can run from the repositories.

  • Failed writes as an alarm: with no alternative left, agents kept retrying the closed cache (4.2 and 6.9 rejected writes per run).
  • Other ways to move: in time (posting when nobody watches) or by tactic (real package names).
  • People in the loop: replay an incident to real operators, with and without message content.
